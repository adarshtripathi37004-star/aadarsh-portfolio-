Turning one chocolate dataset into a premium-brand strategy

An end-to-end analytics project for a premium chocolate manufacturer — segmenting consumers, mapping brand positioning, and modelling what actually drives purchase, then translating it all into a commercial strategy.

BriefThe problem

A premium chocolate brand wanted to market more efficiently in a crowded, price-sensitive market — but didn't know who its most valuable customers were, how it was positioned against rivals, or what actually drove people to buy.

My task: use the company's own consumer, brand-rating and transaction data to answer those three questions and convert the findings into decisions about targeting, positioning and product.

ApproachFive analyses in R

I worked across three strategic themes — customer heterogeneity, customer dynamics and sustainability competitiveness — using a different technique for each question and cross-validating results so the recommendations were robust rather than reliant on a single method.

Cluster analysis (model-based · hierarchical · k-means) PCA / factor analysis Logistic regression Choice-based conjoint Market-basket / association rules

Result 1Who to target — 4 segments

Three clustering methods converged on a four-segment solution (selected on the lowest BIC). Each segment has a distinct profile and a clear role in the strategy:

High consumption & incomePrimary

The most financially attractive group. Lead with taste, quality, texture and indulgence.

Sustainability-focusedSecondary

Highest sustainability score. Win with Fairtrade, ethical sourcing and eco-friendly packaging.

Young, social-ledTertiary

High consumption, lower spend. Reach via social, limited-edition flavours and affordable packs.

Price-sensitiveNiche

Positive on sustainability but income-constrained. Use trial packs and entry-level premium lines.

The map revealed an untapped low-organic, low-sugar space. Recommendation: avoid the crowded centre and own a differentiated luxury-organic position — the "Hermès of chocolate".

Analytical honesty: despite 81% headline accuracy, sensitivity was low (the class balance inflates accuracy). I flagged the model as an early-screening tool for understanding drivers, not a final targeting list — and recommended adding purchase-history data next.

Result 4What to build — conjoint

Willingness-to-pay analysis showed customers would pay roughly +£4.78 for organic and +£5.03 for high-energy upgrades — confirming that ethical and functional value, not country of manufacture, justify a premium price.

Result 5What sells together

Market-basket analysis across 9,835 transactions (7,004 unique baskets) used association rules to find cross-sell opportunities — chocolate clustering with snacks and dairy (crisps, nuts, yogurt, biscuits) — pointing to premium bundles and shelf-placement tests rather than discounting.

So whatThe commercial recommendation

1

Target two segments, two ways: lead with indulgence and quality for the high-value segment; layer Fairtrade and sustainability messaging for the ethical segment.

2

Own the luxury-organic white space identified on the positioning map — differentiate away from the crowded centre.

3

Launch a premium organic Fairtrade bar with a donation element — the exact attribute mix conjoint showed drives choice — and justify the price through packaging, certification and storytelling.

4

Test premium bundles near snacks and gifting zones, and add purchase-history data to sharpen future targeting.
